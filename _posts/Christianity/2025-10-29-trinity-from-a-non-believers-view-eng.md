## 1. Summary of the Parable

In Luke chapter 16, there appears one of Jesus’ most notoriously perplexing teachings: the Parable of the Dishonest (Unjust) Steward. In brief, the story unfolds as follows:

> A property manager (steward) who had been squandering and embezzling a wealthy master's estate was caught red-handed. Announcing his dismissal, the master ordered him to hand over the account books. Stripped of his livelihood, the steward looked for a way to secure favor from others once he was cast out. His scheme was to summon his master's debtors one by one and falsify their bills, reducing the amounts they owed on record. By currying favor with these debtors, he intended to rely on their hospitality later.
>
> Seeing this, the master commended the dishonest steward for acting shrewdly. Jesus then tells his listeners to make friends for themselves by means of unrighteous wealth, so that when it runs out, they may be welcomed into eternal dwellings. He adds that the children of this world are more shrewd in dealing with their own generation than the children of light. He goes on to state that whoever is faithful in a very little is also faithful in much, and that one cannot serve both God and money (Mammon).
{: .prompt-info}

While most of Jesus’ parables are intuitive and easy to grasp, the Parable of the Steward seems almost incoherent at first glance. On the surface, it even appears to endorse self-serving opportunism at any cost, striking readers as profoundly absurd. Why did Jesus share such an anomalous parable?

Perhaps Jesus was putting into practice what is described in Matthew 13:10–17, Mark 4:10–12, and Luke 8:9–10:

> *"To you it has been granted to know the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven, but to them it has not been granted..."*  
> *"Though seeing, they may not see; though hearing, they may not understand, lest they should turn and their sins be forgiven."*

Having yet to encounter a fully satisfying theological explanation of this parable, I have undertaken my own analysis to arrive at an interpretation that is coherent and meaningful.

---

## 2. Traditional Interpretations and Their Flaws

A common traditional interpretation focuses on the master's praise of the steward's cleverness, emphasizing it as an endorsement of the positive aspects of wisdom. Some commentators go so far as to attach a superficial, forced moral virtue to the steward's fraudulent debt reduction, framing it as a "charitable deed to help the needy." Others argue that we must read it through an eschatological lens, claiming the steward's dismissal symbolizes the end times and urges swift, decisive action in preparation for judgment.

However, such interpretations feel like strained rationalizations that merely chase the surface-level wording of the text while missing the core point of the parable.

- Does the master praising shrewdness mean created beings should cleverly commit fraud and breach of trust against God's sovereign authority?
- Does it mean that any act of embezzlement or breach of trust deserves unconditional praise as long as it happens to benefit the poor?
- Are the "children of light" being told to emulate the audacity of committing financial crimes right before facing the Final Judgment?
- Above all, since the impending crisis was brought upon the steward by his own sins in the first place, are we being told to pile more sin on top of sin?

Even a brief reflection reveals these to be fragmented, forced leaps of logic. If anyone were to take such interpretations literally and "shrewdly" commit unrighteousness, they would surely drift far from the secrets of the Kingdom. A person of faith must strive to draw a compelling, cohesive truth despite the parable's inherent difficulty.

A steward is someone entrusted with managing a master’s estate. In this context, the steward likely represents all human beings, who receive their life and existence from God (represented by the master). The narrative of the parable effectively ends halfway through Luke 16:8 with the clause: **"because he had acted shrewdly."** In the latter half of verse 8, Jesus remarks on the "children of light" (those who serve God) and the "children of this world" (those without faith).

**What was the best path the steward should have chosen from the very beginning? It was faithful stewardship, not embezzlement and breach of trust.**

Although the master praised the steward’s shrewdness, he was merely evaluating the man's post-hoc coping mechanism in the face of an already-committed crime and impending termination. The reason the master did not pursue further charges or punishment for reducing the debts was simply because the master was exceptionally magnanimous—not because the steward's actions were legally or morally justified. The master dismissed him, but he did not harbor such hatred as to desire his utter ruin. Had he wished for his ruin, he would have held the steward strictly liable under the law for this new breach of trust.

Rather, the master recognized that the steward, staring into the abyss of a ruined future, refused to give up, exerted desperate efforts to avert catastrophe, and managed to skirt disaster; thus, the master commended his sheer cunning. Under the Law (Exodus 22 and Leviticus 6), the master could have demanded full restitution plus a 20% penalty, or even two- to five-fold restitution, yet he refrained. This reflects the mercy and compassion of God, whom the master represents.

The steward canceled a portion of the debts solely for his own self-interest. The relationship between the steward and the debtors was not founded on righteousness or genuine faithfulness, but strictly on transactional financial gain. The upper limit of goodwill the steward could possibly expect from the debtors was capped at the exact amount he had forgiven: 50 baths of olive oil and 20 cors of wheat. Furthermore, by the time the steward actually needed their help, the debt forgiveness would already be a past event. The debtors would have no compelling reason to remain loyal to a dismissed manager who no longer possessed the authority to remit debts. Therefore, a fraudulent debt reduction that betrayed the master could never secure "eternal dwellings" or lasting friendship. 

Various theological hypotheses have been proposed to resolve this dilemma, but none have successfully untangled the contradictions.

---

### 1) The Social Honor / Reputation Hypothesis

One theory suggests that the master praised the steward because the debt reductions elevated the master’s public reputation as a generous benefactor in the community. This lacks logical coherence. For the master's honor to increase, the decision to remit the debts would have to be perceived as the master's own initiative, reducing the steward to a mere clerk executing orders. If so, there is no explanation for why the debtors would feel personal gratitude toward the dismissed steward and offer *him* shelter.

Furthermore, looking at the actual quantities mentioned in Scripture reveals an absurd scenario. 100 cors of wheat and 100 baths of oil represent massive quantities: a *cor* is approximately 220 liters and a *bath* is roughly 22 liters, totaling 22,000 liters of wheat and 2,200 liters of oil. These were not micro-loans to impoverished peasant farmers, but commercial-scale credit extended to large merchants. Forgiving binding commercial contracts without just cause is not benevolence; it is business folly. Such an arbitrary write-off would severely undermine the master's reputation as a competent estate holder rather than enhance it.

---

### 2) The Illicit Usury (Hidden Interest) Hypothesis

Going a step further than the reputation hypothesis, some scholars propose the "Illicit Usury Theory." According to Deuteronomy 23, charging interest to fellow Israelites was strictly prohibited. Therefore, this theory claims that the amounts written on the bills included illegal usury, and the steward merely deleted that unlawful interest portion.

The logic proceeds as follows: The steward positions himself as a whistleblower to win the debtors’ allegiance. He conspires with them, saying: *"This is illegal interest the master tacked on in violation of the Law. I will strike it from the ledger. Because it violates the Law, the master won't be able to utter a single word of complaint. Don't forget this favor later!"* Handled this way, the master cannot openly demand the interest, because doing so would publicly expose him as a usurious lawbreaker. Gritting his teeth in frustration, the master is forced to save face publicly by grudgingly conceding: **"Well, the fellow certainly acted shrewdly."**

While far more sophisticated than the simple reputation theory, it still suffers from a fatal practical flaw. The goodwill secured through this maneuver is still capped at the one-time value of 50 baths of oil and 20 cors of wheat. That amount cannot purchase an "eternal dwelling." From the debtors' perspective, there is no reason to permanently house and support a disgraced, powerless ex-embezzler whose usefulness has entirely expired.

---

### 3) The Waived Commission Hypothesis: A Complete Impasse

Another popular view argues that, in light of first-century Jewish economic customs and stewardship authority, the forgiven amounts were not the master’s assets, but the steward’s own commission or profit margin. Yet, the reality remains unchanged: the maximum goodwill the steward could purchase was still limited to 50 baths of oil and 20 cors of wheat. Under this interpretation, the steward traded a guaranteed asset (his commission) for the uncertain hope of future hospitality—an act of foolishness rather than shrewdness. This directly contradicts the master's commendation of his wisdom.

Let us examine this "waived commission" theory more closely. Suppose a debtor's total obligation consisted of the master’s principal loan mixed with the steward’s service fee. According to this theory, the steward simply forfeited his personal cut. Why would the steward do this? Let us view the situation from his perspective:

Having been caught embezzling, the master confronts him. The legitimate commissions owed to the steward (50 baths of oil and 20 cors of wheat) would inevitably be seized by the master to offset the embezzled losses. Realizing he would receive nothing anyway, the steward preemptively transfers those assets to the debtors to shield them from seizure, hoping to convert money he was about to lose into social capital (connections). From the master's perspective, this alteration causes no direct financial loss because it leaves the net principal intact while stripping out the steward's fee. Even though the master fails to offset the prior embezzlement, recovering stolen funds has always been notoriously difficult throughout history.

While this logic sounds plausible at first glance, it completely falls apart once we crunch the actual numbers and economic mechanics:

#### (1) Concrete Calculations and Unrealistic Interest Rates:
If the deducted amounts represented the steward's own commission, then out of the total claims (100 baths of oil and 100 cors of wheat), the master's share would be 50 baths of oil and 80 cors of wheat. Based on historical market values of the era, 100 baths of oil were worth approximately 1,100 denarii, and 100 cors of wheat were worth roughly 2,520 denarii. Out of a total value of 3,620 denarii, the value the steward wrote off equals 1,054 denarii. The steward’s commission alone accounts for **29.1%** of the total debt package.

#### (2) The Paradox of Profit Distribution:
For a master to grant an agent a commission larger than his own profit, the steward would have to possess irreplaceable competence. If he were that indispensable, the master would not have summarily fired him for simple mismanagement. It is only reasonable to assume that the master’s share of profit/interest was at least equal to, if not greater than, the steward’s cut. Under that conservative assumption, the total interest component of the debt would be at least double 29.1%, or **58.2%** (rounded to 60% for simplicity). This means a debtor who borrowed 40 units was forced to repay 100 units—an exorbitant interest rate of **150%**. This severely weakens the credibility of the commission hypothesis.

#### (3) Psychological Inconsistency and Wealth Accumulation:
Even under an extreme 150% usurious rate, the steward and the master would be splitting profits almost evenly. For the master to earn more, the interest rate would have to exceed 150%, which defies historical reality. A steward pocketing commissions equal to ~75% of the principal each year would be earning almost as much as the wealthy master himself. Why would such a wealthy individual resort to petty embezzlement? And if he had been skimming extra funds on top of that, he would have accumulated immense personal wealth. It makes no sense that a man of such means would despair over ending up homeless on the street, begging debtors for shelter.

#### (4) The Fallacy of the "Perishability / Risk Asset" Hypothesis:
Another flaw in this theory is its reliance on arbitrary, ad-hoc justifications without documentary evidence. Proponents argue that wheat was a safe asset with high storability (yielding a lower 20% commission), whereas oil was a perishable, volatile risk asset prone to spoilage and evaporation (yielding a higher 50% commission). Commentators then marvel at how "economically sophisticated" the biblical parable supposedly is.

Yet this contradicts basic economics. Interest rates and risk premiums are determined by the **creditworthiness and default risk of the borrower**, not the physical storability of the commodity. Both wheat and oil are consumables; the true risk lies in whether the debtor has the capacity and willingness to repay tomorrow what they consumed today. Would a loan of wheat to a fraudster be safer than a loan of oil to a wealthy Roman general?

Furthermore, if oil carried a significantly higher interest rate, a rational merchant needing oil would simply borrow wheat, sell it in the marketplace to buy oil, and later repay the debt in wheat. Arbitrage would naturally drive down the interest rate on oil until it converged with that of wheat.

#### (5) The "Zero Principal" Paradox (The Master as an Outright Bandit):
Looking closer at the oil debtor: If the commission theory holds, this debtor was paying a 50% commission to the manager. That leaves 50 baths for the master. If the master’s profit must be at least equal to the agent's commission, the master’s interest portion must also be 50 baths. 
$$\text{Steward's Commission (50)} + \text{Master's Interest (50)} = 100 \text{ baths.}$$
**This leaves the principal at exactly zero.** The debtor, required to repay 100 baths of oil, would have borrowed literally nothing. In this scenario, the master is not even a lender; he is an outright bandit.

Thus, while the "waived commission" theory seems sophisticated on the surface, it collapses like a house of cards the moment real numbers and economic logic are applied.

---

### 4) The Eschatological Readiness Hypothesis

Others view the steward's dismissal as an allegory for physical death and the Final Judgment, interpreting the parable as a call to prepare for the end. However, this creates a major category error: death and divine judgment occur according to God's sovereign timetable, whereas the steward's dismissal was purely the consequence of his own criminal misdeeds. 

As demonstrated, existing alternative interpretations remain superficial, missing the core point or selectively isolating fragments of the parable to force a fit.

## 3. A Proposed Alternative Interpretation

Jesus viewed the wealth accumulated by a steward while alienated from his master as inherently transient and limited. He told his disciples to use unrighteous wealth to make friends, so that when it runs out, they might be welcomed into eternal dwellings. However, as demonstrated above, anyone who attempts this in practice will find that worldly debtors cannot provide an eternal dwelling.

The only "eternal dwelling" the steward (humanity) truly needs is not the debtors (money), but the **Master (God)**. In this parable, the truly shrewd course of action for the steward would be to fall face down before the exceptionally generous master—who forgave even his final act of breach of trust—sincerely repent, seek mercy, and restore the broken relationship. The true meaning of "making friends with unrighteous wealth to secure an eternal dwelling" is not an endorsement to distribute dirty money as bribes to curry worldly favor.

---

### 1) Who is the True "Friend" Jesus Refers To?
Who is the "friend" Jesus tells his disciples to make? As established, nothing lasting can be gained from the debtors. Eliminating the debtors leaves only one person in the narrative: the **Master**. 

Relying solely on the process of elimination might seem questionable. Yet, as if fitting the final piece into a puzzle, Jesus explicitly addresses his disciples in Luke 12:4 and John 15:15 not as servants, but as **friends**. Therefore, the most natural and contextually coherent interpretation is that disciples should return ill-gotten wealth to the Master, thereby restoring trust and true friendship with Him.

---

### 2) The Plural "Friends" and a Practical Interpretation via "Love of Neighbor"
Some might object to identifying the "friend" as the Master because the Greek text uses the plural form (*philous*, "friends"). This can be resolved through a practical reading centered on the command to "love one's neighbor."

Equating the debtors directly with the "friends" is logically untenable, as relationships built on transaction are fleeting and cannot guarantee an eternal dwelling. Therefore, we must understand the orientation of friendship as centered on the Master, and consider what it means to use wealth for the Master's sake:

#### (1) Serving God Through Loving Neighbors:
According to Matthew 22:37–40, the Master (God) regards loving one's neighbor as using wealth in direct obedience to His command. Furthermore, in Matthew 25:40, Jesus declares: *"Whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers and sisters of mine, you did for me."* Loving one's neighbor is inseparable from serving God.

#### (2) Fruit Flowing from Restored Relationship:
Even if wealth was acquired through unrighteous means, using it for neighbors in accordance with the Master’s will makes those neighbors "friends." Through this obedience rooted in love of neighbor, one ultimately receives the "eternal dwelling"—salvation granted by God. Rather than a scheme of relying on debtors through fraud, this represents the truly wise path of loving neighbors through the Master. The beneficiaries might even include the debtors; however, when given through the Master rather than as a transactional bribe, it becomes genuine love shared with friends. Here, loving one's neighbor is not a transactional "means to earn salvation," but the **natural fruit that overflows from a restored relationship with the Master—God, the ultimate Friend.**

#### (3) Divine Hospitality Revealed Through Neighborly Hospitality:
For those who feel uncomfortable applying the title of "friend" directly to God despite Jesus' invitation, an alternative perspective is possible: "Friends" refers to neighbors (especially the poor), and "their welcoming you" reflects God's divine hospitality manifested through the hospitality of the neighbor. In short, the narrative flows: **"First return to the Master and restore your relationship with Him; then, make friends by sharing with neighbors according to the Master's will."**

---

### 3) Children of Light vs. Children of this World
Let us follow Jesus' words further. The children of light are often not as shrewd as the children of this world (represented by the dishonest steward). When people place their faith in God, they often lack the fierce desperation and industrious zeal shown by the steward in his crisis.

The children of light must shake off complacency and learn from the passion, diligence, and tenacity with which worldly people navigate their daily lives. If the children of light serve God with that level of earnestness and fierce commitment, God will entrust them with far greater responsibilities. Whoever is faithful in small things can be trusted with great things. Jesus urges his disciples to serve God with the same intensity that worldly people pursue wealth—yet, knowing that one cannot serve both God and money, they must choose God and remain true children of light.

---

### 4) A Historical Contrast: The Pharisees vs. Zacchaeus the Chief Tax Collector (Luke 19)
The Pharisees, who loved money and needed this teaching most, scoffed at the parable. To them, piety and wealth were entirely compatible. Under their interpretation of Deuteronomy 28, following God brought material wealth, while poverty implied disobedience; thus, wealth was a badge of honor, and poverty hinted at sin.

Jesus pierced through their hypocrisy by telling the story of the rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19–31), urging their spiritual awakening. If these Pharisees—who lived with the fierce drive of the steward—were to become true children of light, their spiritual potential would be immense, qualifying them for great kingdom missions. Yet, their lack of response indicates that they heard without understanding, failing to attain repentance. Lacking faith in Jesus, they dismissed his teaching on the steward as rambling nonsense.

In contrast, even if Jesus' disciples did not grasp the full meaning immediately, they trusted that their Master—who spoke only truth—did not utter vain words. They meditated on it and sought understanding, drawing closer to the secrets of the Kingdom.

For future generations who would encounter Jesus only through the written text, Luke provided a crucial hermeneutical key in Luke 19. There, Jesus tells the Parable of the Ten Minas—a story highlighting the wisdom and diligent effort required of God's servants. Right before that parable, Luke presents the account of **Zacchaeus the chief tax collector**:

- Desiring to see Jesus but prevented by his short stature and the pressing crowd,
- Zacchaeus climbed a sycamore-fig tree, expressing his desperate yearning—an act that pleased Jesus.
- Having likely heard reports of Jesus telling the rich ruler to give all his possessions to the poor (Luke 18:22), Zacchaeus took the initiative before Jesus even spoke, pledging to give half of his possessions to the poor and repay fourfold anyone he had defrauded.

As a chief tax collector, Zacchaeus was the very embodiment of **"unrighteous wealth."** Yet, he **used that unrighteous wealth for his friends—his neighbors and the poor.** Jesus rejoiced, declaring salvation to his household even though Zacchaeus offered half rather than the entirety of his wealth. Luke presents Zacchaeus as the consummate role model: a devout child of light who acted with the shrewd, decisive wisdom of the steward.

### * A Trinitarian Reflection on the Plural "Friends"

The question of the plural "friends" can also be approached from a theological perspective. Within Christian orthodoxy, referring to God in the plural is entirely coherent with the doctrine of the Trinity.

In Genesis 1:26, God says, *"Let Us make man in Our image, according to Our likeness."* While the historical-critical consensus often interprets this as the divine council speaking with angels, a long-standing Christian tradition reads it as an early glimpse of the Trinity. From a believer's perspective, the Council of Nicaea did not invent the Trinity; it merely **discovered and affirmed a truth that existed from the beginning.** 

Jesus, as the incarnate Second Person of the Trinity, could naturally speak of the divine Godhead (Father, Son, and Holy Spirit) in the plural. Recognizing traces of this plural reality throughout Scripture can be a deeply profound theological reflection. One might imagine the following historical dialogue:

> **Luke:** "You said *friends* will welcome them? Not a single friend?"  
> **Simon Peter:** "Yes. It sounded unusual to me as well. But I am recounting precisely what I heard from the Lord. How could I alter His exact words simply because my mind cannot fully fathom His deeper meaning?"

## 4. What Should We Learn from Jesus’ Parable of the Unjust Steward?
